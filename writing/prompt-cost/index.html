Why I’m running the worse version of my product on purpose

Matt Thomas · November 2026

I made my product better, tested it, and then chose not to implement it.

The reason was cost. If you run anything on an AI model, you get offered the same trade constantly: a bigger model, a longer prompt, more reasoning. Each one makes the work a little better and the bill a little bigger, and it’s easy to say yes every time, because the improvement is easy to see and the cost isn’t. Saying no takes one number: what the upgrade adds to the cost of each finished piece of work. Here’s how that number played out for my own product.

The product

Byte Sized AI News is a YouTube channel that runs by itself. I had about 20 AI newsletters in my inbox that I never read, so I built something to read them for me.

Every day at noon a job in Google Cloud pulls about 50 stories from the TLDR newsletters, and a decision model ranks them. Only the stories that meet the criteria I set get a video, which works out to anywhere from 10 to 20 a day. Claude writes a 40-second script for each, quoting the source for every line, and a separate fact check goes over every claim. Then an AI voice reads it, a cartoon narrator presents it, and it posts as a YouTube Short.

What one video costs

Almost all of the cost is Claude writing the scripts, about 14 cents per finished video. Rendering is a small flat cost for the whole day, and the voice is free.

The upgrade

I wanted the scripts clearer for people outside tech, and I wanted people to keep watching. One retention guide for YouTube Shorts says 30% to 50% of viewers swipe away in the first three seconds.

So I added rules to the prompt: explain any name the viewer might not know, open on the most concrete fact and never on a question, say why it matters in the second scene, and end on a question someone can answer in a word or two.

The scripts came out clearer, and the cost per video went from about 14 cents to about 22. More rules gave the model more to think through before writing, and that thinking is billed, so output per video went up 46%.

I tried to get that back by turning down the effort setting. Tokens fell 22%, which looked like a win. But drafts passing my checks on the first try fell from 6 in 10 to 3 in 10, and a failed draft gets rewritten. I was paying less per attempt and then paying for more attempts. After that I only looked at cost per finished video.

The decision

The current writer costs about 14 cents a video and the better one about 22. At 10 to 20 videos a day, that difference comes to about $24 to $48 a month. The extra 8 cents buys clearer scripts and, I’d bet, more people watching to the end.

I ranked what the project needs. First, the news has to be reliable. Second, it has to stay affordable, since I pay for it myself every day. Third, it should be polished enough to reach more people. The better writer only improves polish, and it does that at the expense of affordability, which matters more.

So the cheaper writer runs, and every run has a spending cap. 8 cents is small, but 57% more on your main cost is a real decision at any size.

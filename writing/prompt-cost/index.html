I made my prompt better and it cost 57% more

Matt Thomas · November 2026

Recently I wrote a better version of the prompt that runs my YouTube channel, tested it, and then didn’t turn it on. The reason was 8 cents a video.

Some background first. I had about 20 AI newsletters in my inbox that I never actually read, so I built a YouTube channel that reads them for me. It’s called Byte Sized AI News and the entire thing runs by itself.

Every day at noon a job in Google Cloud pulls about 50 stories from the TLDR newsletters. A decision model picks the ones actually worth knowing about. Claude writes a 40-second script for each one and has to quote the source for every line, and then a fact checker and an editor go over it. After that an AI voice reads it, the captions get timed to every word, it’s animated with a cartoon narrator and a graphic for each point, and it uploads itself as a Short. That’s up to 10 videos a day and I don’t touch any of them.

This post is about what one of those videos costs, because that turned out to be the part of the project I understood the least going in, and it’s the reason the better prompt is still sitting there unused.

What a video costs

You’d think rendering would be the expensive part, since it’s video. It’s about 35 cents for a whole day of videos on Cloud Run, and that’s an estimate at list price because it hasn’t shown up on a bill yet. The voice is an open-source model, so there’s no bill for that either.

Almost all of the money goes to Claude writing the scripts, which works out to about 14 cents per finished video. When I looked at the token counts, about 65% of the output I was being billed for was reasoning, meaning the thinking the model does before it writes anything. I never see that text and it never ends up in a video, but it costs the same as the script does.

Adding rules

The scripts were okay, but I wanted two things from them. I wanted them clearer, so someone who isn’t in tech could follow a story without getting lost. And I wanted people to actually keep watching, which on Shorts is hard. The research I did on the format says about 30 to 50% of viewers swipe away in the first three seconds.

So I added rules to the prompt. Some were for clarity: explain any name the viewer might not know, keep it to one idea per sentence, don’t add drama the story doesn’t have. The rest were for engagement. The first line has to state the news with the most concrete fact up front, and it can’t be a question. The second scene has to say why the viewer should care. If the story has a catch, the script hints at it early and pays it off about three quarters of the way through. And the video ends on a question someone can answer in a word or two, instead of “like and subscribe.”

The scripts came out clearer. The cost per video also went from about 14 cents to about 22.

The extra cost wasn’t really the longer prompt itself. It was that the model now had more rules to think through before it wrote anything, so the output per video went up 46%, and most of that output is the reasoning I was already paying for and can’t see.

Trying to get the 8 cents back

I tried a few things here and most of them didn’t work.

The first was turning down the effort setting, which controls how hard the model thinks. That cut tokens by 22%, which looked great until I checked how many drafts were passing my checks on the first try. It went from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10. A draft that fails gets sent back and rewritten, so I was saving money on each attempt and then paying for more attempts.

The second was using Haiku, the smaller and cheaper model, to write the drafts. With thinking turned off it couldn’t count or keep track of several rules at once, and with thinking on it would just keep reasoning and never finish.

I also tried slimming down the output format by making some of the fields optional, and the API refused it outright with “Schema is too complex.”

The one thing that did work was pretty small. On-screen text has a character limit, and when a line runs over, something has to shorten it. That job doesn’t need the big model, so Haiku does it now, and if that fails there’s a plain code trim as a backup.

What I decided

So right now I have two versions of the writer. The old one costs about 14 cents a video. The new one, with the clarity and engagement rules, costs about 22, and it’s sitting in the main branch, tested and ready to go.

The live job is still running the old one.

It’s not that I can’t find out whether the new one does better. YouTube gives me a dashboard for that, and I’d bet the new rules do perform better. It’s that views aren’t my first priority right now. My first priority is keeping this thing sustainable. I pay for it myself and it runs every day, so it has to stay affordable while still putting out something I’d stand behind. For a news channel that means the news has to be reliable.

And that part doesn’t change between the two writers. Both go through the same fact check, where every line has to quote the story and a second model checks every claim. So running the cheaper writer doesn’t make the videos less accurate. It makes them a little less polished, and that’s a trade I’m okay with for now.

Eight cents is obviously not a lot of money. At 10 videos a day it’s around $24 a month. But it’s a 57% increase on the main cost of the project, and it would be going to the thing that’s third on my list, after reliable and affordable. So the cheaper writer runs, every run has a spending cap, and the docs say that the live job isn’t on the newest code.

The next thing I want to try is the Batch API, which is half price if you can wait up to an hour or so for the results. The job runs at noon and nobody is sitting there waiting on it, so that could bring the better writer down to around what the old one costs. I haven’t tested it yet, but if it works I don’t have to choose.

What I took from it

There are two ways to describe a project like this. One is what it does, which is the fun part to build and to talk about. The other is what one finished piece of work costs and what you get for it.

Most of what I see written about AI projects only does the first one. But the second one is what decides whether the thing keeps running. For me the unit is a video and the numbers are in cents. At a company it would be an insurance claim processed or an audit finished, and there’d be a lot more zeros, but it’s the same question: is the better version worth what it costs, given what you actually need right now? If I’m ever on the other side of that conversation, the cost per finished unit is the first number I’d ask for.
